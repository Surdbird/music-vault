The City in the Imagination
In the manner of Pico Iyer
✦ Sanctorum
"There are cities you leave and cities that leave you. Calcutta, for those who grew up there and then went away, appears to be the second kind."

There are cities you leave and cities that leave you. Calcutta, for those who grew up there and then went away, appears to be the second kind. You leave Calcutta in the way that you leave a long dream — gradually, reluctantly, still half inside it, turning back at intervals to check whether it has changed or whether you have. The answer, as a rule, is both.

— ✦ —

Tufan wrote something this week in response to Sujit's piece on John Mayer's Calcutta-Nagar — eighteen piano sketches of a city recalled from exile — that stopped me in my tracks. He wrote about Nizam's kathi rolls paid for in school pocket change, the waiter's resignation as the coins came out. About his mother shopping at New Market with him trailing behind in awestruck wonder. About the hand-pulled rickshaw clapper still audible in North Calcutta, in the neighbourhood where he still has a house. About Pam Crain and her husband Don, friends. About Carlton Kitto playing jazz to an empty bar in the early nineties, the audience for his kind of music measurably shrunk, sometimes Tufan the only one requesting tunes.

And at the end, almost parenthetically: "I played my tenor sax on stage the day before yesterday with Braz Gonsalves in Goa at Take 5 in Candolim. It was quite the moment. I played badly by my reckoning. I was nervous. But still…"

But still. There is a whole philosophy in those two words. The city you carry inside you does not ask for perfection. It asks only that you show up.

— ✦ —

John Mayer's sketches — the eighteen miniatures, some of them barely twenty seconds long — do what the best city writing does: they don't try to capture everything. They catch a quality of light, a sound at a particular hour, the way a market smells in the morning. Mayer was Anglo-Indian, his relationship to Calcutta necessarily at an angle — insider and outsider at once, the perspective that sometimes sees most clearly precisely because it cannot take the city for granted. Tufan, reading Sujit's prose and listening to the music, finds himself somewhere between recognition and loss. "These musical etudes were small jewels burnished and glowing, just in order to catalyze buried memories." He is describing the music. He is also describing what it is to belong to a place you no longer live in.

— ✦ —

Then Tufan found the vinyl. In a scene from Ray's Aranyer Din Ratri — Days and Nights in the Forest, 1970 — a shelf of records is visible. Mozart. Segovia. Rubber Soul. And the Indo-Jazz Suite by Joe Harriott and John Mayer. Another connection, unexpected, across fifty years. Dr. Adi Gazder — paediatrician, pianist, member of the Calcutta Gramophone Society with Ray — was almost certainly the source. He would have been a contemporary of Mayer. The city folds back on itself. The musicians knew each other. They always do.

The blues video. Members of this group — VP Krishnan, Robu, Jhumka — playing a Hindi song rendered in blues. The blues phrasing found the longing that was always in it. There are songs that wait for the right register to release what they contain. This was one of them.

— ✦ —

Ahmad Jamal's birthday this week. He was a newspaper boy at seven, delivering papers to Billy Strayhorn's family home in Pittsburgh. The master of space and silence, selling newspapers to the family of the master of voice and colour. The city is always smaller than it looks. The connections are always closer than they appear. You just have to know where to look — or in Tufan's case, to pause a film at exactly the right moment and read the spines.

Abdullah Ibrahim died on Monday. He was ninety-one. He had been carrying Cape Town inside him since before most of us were born, and he carried it to the end, which is more than most people manage with anything.

Tufan saw him play at the Dalhousie Institute in Calcutta, sometime around 1989. Tall and patrician in a long flowing robe. Rich and melodious, Tufan said. Desirous and encouraging of contemplation, limned with bursts of utter joy. That was his entrepôt to another continent's music. Suranjan was there too, as it turned out. Neither of them had known the other was in the room. This is the kind of thing you find out thirty-five years later in a WhatsApp chat at four in the afternoon, and then you sit with it for a moment before the next link arrives.

Tufan said: everybody leaves Calcutta, but does Calcutta ever leave 'em?

Nobody answered. This was the correct response.

So it goes.

The Unopened Letter
In the manner of Geoff Dyer
✦ Sanctorum
"The fact I cannot stop thinking about, from this week, is that Art Davis — bassist on A Love Supreme, sued the New York Philharmonic over racial discrimination in 1969 — earned a doctorate in clinical psychology and ran a private practice helping other musicians manage emotional stress."

The fact I cannot stop thinking about, from this week, is that Art Davis — bassist on A Love Supreme, sued the New York Philharmonic over racial discrimination in 1969, played on the records I would take to a desert island — earned a doctorate in clinical psychology and ran a private practice helping other musicians manage emotional stress. I keep trying to picture the office. Did anyone walking in recognise him? Or had they only heard the name, vaguely, the way you hear most names, and only afterwards thought: hang on, Art Davis, that Art Davis? I imagine they sat down, said the thing they had come to say, the small unbearable thing about not being able to sleep before a session, or about the woman who had left in February, and Davis listened the way he must have listened on the bandstand — which is to say, listened as the work itself. The chat this week, I realised somewhere around Friday afternoon, kept turning up versions of this same thought: that musicians have second lives, third lives, often more interesting than the ones the album sleeves tell you about, and that this is so much the rule that calling it a hidden life almost misses the point. Sterling Morrison of the Velvet Underground wrote his dissertation on Cynewulf, the Anglo-Saxon poet. Jeff "Skunk" Baxter from Steely Dan became a consultant on missile defence. Bhupen Hazarika went from Columbia, where his thesis was about audiovisual techniques in adult education, to writing the songs about the Brahmaputra. Sujit asked these as quiz questions — they were answered fast, by people who had clearly been keeping these facts the way you keep an unopened letter you don't quite want to open — and then we moved on.

— ✦ —

The kind of taxonomy that the rest of us recognise as both an act of generosity and a quiet declaration. He started walking, in the heat, to Pops's room, every day, hoping to find him in and prevailed upon to lend it. This continued for some weeks. He has not said how many. Late on Sunday afternoon, Tufan posted Dylan's Everything Is Broken, a live recording, and noted that surely Dylan must be reading the lyrics off a prompter. But then why the shades? Wouldn't they make it harder? Or has he memorised all those lines, which would itself be a feat? Parama, with the calm of someone who has been thinking about this question for forty years, answered: "He knows his lines, and if he doesn't, what he sings anew become the lines." This is the kind of remark that solves more questions than the one being asked.

— ✦ —

Mukund posted, near the very end of Sunday, a passage about being eighty — he saw it somewhere, he said. The best thing about being eighty, the passage claimed, is that you outlive the clocks that have been chasing you. The worst thing is that you have, at last, an understanding of something that might have altered everything, had it come at a time when something could still be altered. When you're young you think time moves forward. At eighty you know it stands still. We're the ones that move. It is a beautiful passage. It is also, as I discovered a few hours later, Bob Dylan, writing that same Sunday in The New York Times, on the occasion of another man's eightieth birthday. He'd been asked to give the man some advice. He had declined the advice and offered this instead. So the week, on second look, turns out to have been about Dylan all along — the shades, the prompter, Everything Is Broken, the lines that become themselves anew, and now this final, anonymous-looking passage that Mukund posted at five to midnight and that the chat read, very briefly, as wisdom from no one in particular before scrolling on. Sterling Morrison's Cynewulf, Art Davis listening at the desk, Vignesh arriving late to a band he had loved for years without knowing he had — every hidden life turning out, the moment you read it twice, to have been hiding in plain sight. We're the ones that move. The passage was always Dylan. We just hadn't quite recognised the voice.

3. The ten-thousandth listen.

Raj, Saturday: a quiz question. When were the words "Purple Rain" first used in a song, before Prince? The questions are different now, the prizes are smaller, nobody is foaming at the mouth, and the room is global instead of being in Joka. But it is the same quiz. Music Only is what happens when the quiz won't end and the players don't want it to.

A Hundred Years
In the manner of Miles Davis — on his centenary
✦ Sanctorum
"Yeah. A hundred years. They been talking about me all week. Centenary this, centenary all that. Like I give a shit. I didn't give a shit when I was alive and I don't give one now. Hundred years ain't shit. Bird only got to thirty-four. Trane got to forty. Wayne almost made eighty-nine. Sonny made ninety-five. Sonny just made it out the door this week..."

Yeah. A hundred years. They been talking about me all week. Centenary this, centenary all that. Like I give a shit. I didn't give a shit when I was alive and I don't give one now. Hundred years ain't shit. Bird only got to thirty-four. Trane got to forty. Wayne almost made eighty-nine. Sonny made ninety-five. Sonny just made it out the door this week, motherfuckers. Last cat standing in Art Kane's photograph and then he wasn't standing no more. Hari said it right — one morning you find Sonny Rollins has also gone off to play some celestial gig. That's the only obit worth a damn.

Sonny. Newk. Brother of mine. I had two tenor players in my band for a minute — Sonny and Trane, both at the same goddamn time — and I'd play the head and then I'd sit down and watch them fight it out. Jimmy Cobb said I was evil like that. Maybe I was. But you wanted to know who could play. You put two cats with that much shit in their horn on the same bandstand and you find out fast. Sonny was beating up on Trane every night that year. So Trane started practicing. Morning, noon, and night. Trane found his shit because Sonny was making him look for it. Necessity, motherfucker. That's how it works. I knew what I was doing.

— ✦ —

Read what Mike LeDonne wrote about playing with Sonny — the energy he put out could power the entire world. Mike's right. You ever stood next to Sonny when he was blowing? Your knees shake. Somebody had to keep Sonny up in the chat all week and y'all did. Sujit wrote a beautiful thing in The Wire about how Sonny found his way through India. Yoga. Patanjali. Used yoga as his secret weapon to master the saxophone. Sonny did, brother. He went up on that Williamsburg Bridge in '59 and practiced for three years till the cops thought he was a jumper. Came back with a different horn. Different man. That's what India does. That's what the bridge does. Same thing.

I had Sonny on Bags' Groove. His tunes — Airegin, Oleo, Doxy — his playing, his shit. Parama posted Worktime. Suman posted the Guardian list of the greatest. Yeah it was.

And while we on Elvin — Joe Farrell. Sanjay and Parama been keeping Farrell up for a minute and the chat noticed I been too quiet about it. Cat played with everybody. McLaughlin called him for My Goal's Beyond. Elvin had him on his own records, and Farrell's own Outback on CTI had Elvin guesting back — Bharath knew. Then Toussaint put him in the five-horn section on The Band's Rock of the Ages, tenor and soprano and English horn — Sanjay caught that one. English horn, motherfucker. That's a cat who goes where the music's interesting and leaves when it ain't.

Now your boy Dylan turned eighty-five last Sunday and the chat got to celebrating that too. I never said much about Dylan. He was a poet. I was a horn player. Different rooms, same building, you understand? Adi posted a twenty-minute thing about how the desert makes one kind of music and the swamp makes another. Now that's a smart motherfucker. That's the thing nobody talks about. The room makes the music. The night air makes the music. Where you standing when you breathing in — that makes the music. You don't get Kind of Blue in a swamp. You don't get Smokestack Lightning in a Manhattan studio. The place plays the cat. The cat just holds the horn.

— ✦ —

And then the chat got into the funk thing. Cissy Strut. The Meters. Robu wrote a goddamn dissertation. Said funk lives on the one. Said Zigaboo Modeliste is a fucking genius. Said the song is just C minor and C minor seven and the drums make the song. Robu's right and Robu's wrong. Robu's right about Zigaboo. Robu's right about the one. But it ain't just the drums. It's the air around the drums. It's the silence between the snare and the kick. Same shit as jazz, brother. Same shit as everything. He lets it go, somebody said about Ahmad Jamal last week. Yeah. He lets it go. That's what Red Garland did for me. That's what Zigaboo does for the Meters. Funk and swing ain't different rooms. They're just different temperatures.

Tufan got into it with ChatGPT about whether funk swings. Tufan, brother — you can argue with the computer all day. Funk swings when it wants to. Don't swing when it don't. Ain't no rules. I told 'em that in '69 and they still arguing about it in '26. Some things never change.

A small glossary, for those who'd like one. The non-English words are mostly isiZulu and isiXhosa, the two big Nguni languages of South Africa, which Masekela switched between freely. A few are Cape or Afrikaans-derived.

mfowethu — isiZulu, "my brother" (literally "brother of ours"). Affectionate, used between men.

Yebo — isiZulu, "Yes." Often used as an emphatic "yeah, exactly."

Hayi — isiZulu/isiXhosa, "No" or "Hey!" Here a mild exclamation, like "Oh, this boy" (gently exasperated).

Yhuu — isiXhosa exclamation. Roughly "Whew!" or "Oh man!" An expression of awe, weariness, or amazement. Hugh used this constantly.

bantfwana — isiZulu/siSwati, "children" or "young ones." Used affectionately for younger members of the group who need things explained.

isicathamiya — isiZulu, the a cappella vocal style Ladysmith Black Mambazo sing. Literally "stalking gently" — refers to the soft-footed dance moves done while singing, originally so as not to disturb the camp guards in the mining hostels.

kwela — South African township jive music of the '50s, pennywhistle-driven. The word comes from isiZulu for "climb on" or "get up" — also slang for the police vans, so the music had a watchful, "look out" connotation.

shebeen — Hiberno-English origin (from the Irish síbín, an illicit unlicensed pub), absorbed into South African English to mean the township taverns where home-brewed beer was sold. The word travelled from the Irish potato famine to the South African gold mines via the same colonial machinery.

bantu — literally just "people" in Nguni languages (the singular is umuntu). The word is loaded in South Africa because the apartheid regime weaponised it as a racial category ("Bantu education," etc). Masekela reclaims it to mean simply "people, my people."

heyi — isiZulu/isiXhosa, "hey!" — exclamation of mild surprise or emphasis.

Sala kahle — isiZulu, "Stay well." Said by the person leaving.

Hamba kahle — isiZulu, "Go well." Said by the person staying. Together they're the standard call-and-response farewell pair — like "vaya con dios" and its answer.
