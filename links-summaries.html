Still Grazing
In the manner of Hugh Masekela
✦ Sanctorum

Who says a 1-4-5 chord progression has to be boring! Yebo, mfowethu, who indeed. Monday Robu posts "Spirit Voices" and he's not even thinking about it, he's just walking in the evening and he's noticed Bakithi Kumalo's bass for the thousandth time. Who says a 1-4-5 chord progression has to be boring! Yebo, mfowethu, who indeed. Then Adi comes in with the runner story I have heard Bakithi tell himself maybe twenty times and never the same way twice. The teenager in the township. The job as a runner for a white mechanic. Bass at work for lunch-break practice. The boss calls him in and Bakithi thinks he's getting fired. Do you know who Paul Simon is? No. Isn't Paul Simon Jamaican? Hayi, this boy. Off he goes on the bus, no bass case, greasy hands, ripped pants, toes sticking out of his shoes. Paul sends somebody out to the music store and comes back with a Fender. That Fender plays the Zimbabwe shows. Then it gets stolen back home, because of course it does. South Africa, mfowethu. The country gives you a thing and then it takes it back.

— ✦ —

Now I want to be honest with you about Paul Simon. There was a time some of us in exile could not say his name without spitting. The cultural boycott was sacred. Graceland came right through it, with our musicians on it and our music on it, and a credit dispute that took twenty years to sort out properly. Some of those brothers had nothing in their fridges. They needed those royalties yesterday, not in twenty years. But here is the other side and you can't pretend it isn't there. Bakithi plays on that record and the whole world finds out a township boy can outplay anybody anywhere. Ladysmith goes from rehearsing in a Zulu hostel to Carnegie Hall. Kids in Memphis and Paris and Bombay hear isicathamiya for the first time in their lives. Tufan posts the Malcolm McLaren — who ripped off whom? Parama asks. Yhuu, brother, you could write a book. Some of us did. The answer is everybody ripped off everybody and some of us also gave it away. That is the music business. That is also the world.

— ✦ —

Then Tuesday night Ravi steps in and the man knows what he is talking about. He was in Pretoria and Joburg around Madiba's swearing-in in '94. Plays at the Market Theatre. Gigs in shebeens. Now there is a word that needs explaining for the bantfwana in the group. After the Boer War the mines needed black labour, and the Dominion government made home brewing illegal so the mining houses could open licensed shebeens on the compounds and sell the workers their own beer back at a profit. That is where marabi was born — township piano music, the great-grandfather of kwela, the grandfather of Cape jazz, the father of me and Dollar and Miriam and Caiphus. Crime makes music sometimes. Apartheid was a crime that made a lot of music.

— ✦ —

Then Ravi posts the one that matters. Mannenberg. Oh, brother. Yhuu. Mannenberg was the Cape Town suburb that refused to sort itself by colour. Black, Coloured, white, Malay people who had been there since the Dutch — all mixed together in one place like they had no manners. The Boers hated it. So they tried to break it. In 1974 brother Dollar — by then he was already Abdullah Ibrahim, but to me he is still Dollar from the Jazz Epistles days in '59, '60 — Dollar writes a tune and calls it "Mannenberg Is Where It's At." Basil Coetzee comes in on the tenor and plays a solo so long and so wounded and so right that for the rest of his life the man was Basil Mannenberg. The piano figure on that recording, Ravi rightly notes, has its bones in the Indonesian music brought to the Cape by people the Dutch took out of Java in chains. Apartheid wanted everything separate. The music kept stitching it back together. We took "Mannenberg" as a signature. We played it at rallies. We played it in London. We played it because it said we were already a country, and the country they had built was the fiction.

Tufan, who saw Dollar play in Calcutta and never forgot it, calls it right when he says patrician elegant. That is exactly what Dollar was. Sat at the piano like a priest. Wore his suits like vestments. Played as if he had all the time in the world and the world had better wait. He had it because he had earned it. He left when he had to. He came back when he could.

Then Bharath asks the question we have all been waiting for him to ask. How did Manzarek get in that photo? And he posts Pukwana's Diamond Express, and the Zimbabwe remasters. So I will say it plainly: if you are coming to South African jazz late, do not start anywhere except with the Blue Notes. Chris McGregor at the piano, Dudu Pukwana on alto, Mongezi Feza on pocket trumpet — yhuu, what a player, gone at thirty — Johnny Dyani on bass, Louis Moholo on drums, Ronnie Beer on tenor. Mixed-race band in apartheid South Africa. They couldn't gig in their own country. They left in '64 for Antibes and never came home. Most of them are dead now. Listen to Blue Notes for Mongezi. Then listen to Mannenberg. Then go back to Graceland and tell me what you hear.

— ✦ —

A few other things happened this week, bantu, and I should mention them or Tufan will say I missed something. Stevie turned 76 on Wednesday and Suranjan posted "Living for the City." Stevie was always our brother. Came down in '79 when nobody else would. Russell Malone is gone — died on the road with brother Ron Carter. Sometimes the road takes you. Alla put it best about Ron: zero ego. That is the man. Mavis was teaching Wilco the harmonies in rehearsal and Adi caught it on tape — Pops Staples is family of mine, going back. There was a beautiful long thread about Bob's records on Free School Street and Sudder Street in Calcutta, Ash and Ravi and Tufan and Manivannan all turning up at the same hot July counter in 1985 and 1991 and never quite at the same time, scoring vinyl and other things, the merch-wallahs and the music-wallahs and the PYTs — heyi, the world is small. Anin posted the whole Festival Express story — Janis and Jerry on the bar car at sixty miles an hour, the liquor store in Saskatoon, the best time I've had since Port Arthur — three months later she was gone. Sometimes the train gives you back what the world has taken, and sometimes that is the last thing it gives you.

— ✦ —

There was a sweet moment too in the middle of all of it. Parama posts an old photograph of friends from Madras days, and writes that he and Arvind were dipping into a shared past, and Nandu and Ajay and Sundar and KK are all gone now, was in another lifetime of course a camaraderie long forgotten I thought until Music Only rekindled it. Sumit replies had me at 'twas in another lifetime. Sukumar comes in with the fields are full of dancing, full of singing and romancing. That is what this group is for, brothers and sisters. People who are gone and people who are here. Music as a way of holding on.

— ✦ —

A long argument also broke out about whether all jazz swings, started by Sanjay, finished by nobody, with Tufan eventually noting that Latin jazz doesn't depend on swung triplets and Hariharan declaring it's all Latin to me and Adi quoting Ella — it don't mean a thing — and Ravi delivering the line of the week: to listen to Bach is to hear a man think. Yes, brother. Yes, it is. And to listen to Dollar is to hear a man pray. And to listen to Bakithi is to hear a man who came in with toes sticking out of his shoes and walked out with a Fender.

— ✦ —

I am still grazing, bantu. Still grazing on all this music you good people throw on the field every week. Tufan said the other day that this group is fairly egoless, and Alla said we are old, and Tufan said odd, and Alla said also. Yebo. All of it. Sala kahle, music people. Hamba kahle. Until next Sunday.

The Claude works in mysterious ways.

Week 19 4–10 May 2026
The Affair of the New Arrival
In the manner of P.G. Wodehouse
✦ Sanctorum
"The Music Only club, that cherished gathering place of musical chappies and chappesses, spent the week in unusually elevated spirits, and I don't mind telling you the cause. Into our midst — the way a particularly fine spring breeze arrives unannounced and rearranges the curtains in the morning room — came a new member..."

The Music Only club, that cherished gathering place of musical chappies and chappesses, spent the week in unusually elevated spirits, and I don't mind telling you the cause. Into our midst — the way a particularly fine spring breeze arrives unannounced and rearranges the curtains in the morning room — came a new member. Her name is Autumn Hazel Luna, and her introductory dispatch was, in Raj Nair's view, the best intro ever in the group. It was, to use the Wooster expression, the goods. Brought in by Parama following what she described, with admirable economy, as a Phishy weekend in Vegas a few weeks back, Autumn proceeded to lay out her musical curriculum vitae with the candour of one who knows precisely what she is about. Colorado in the nineties (Dead, Lauryn Hill, Neil Young). A spell in Mexico (accordion-driven Norteño, the most closeted compartment of my musical tastes, to be honest). Spain in summer for flamenco. Long stretches in Oman, where she fell unexpectedly for habibi funk and oud-driven Levantine folk. Home base: Soul, Funk, Folk, Bluegrass, Classic Rock and lately Jazz. I LOVE me some Prince, she added, in case anyone was harbouring doubts on that front. The reception was, to deploy the technical term, gushing. Krish welcomed. Mukund welcomed. Robu welcomed. Bharath, with the air of a chap who cannot resist the well-laid trap, observed that her devotion to The Band must surely centre on Richard Manuel — and come to think of it, they do sing about the cows coming home too. Parama duly produced the lyrical evidence, and Arjun Mitra, ever a man for the apt citation, supplied Frank Zappa on the matter: Information is not knowledge. Knowledge is not wisdom. Wisdom is not truth. Truth is not beauty. Beauty is not love. Love is not music. Music is the best. It was generally agreed that the club had done well by itself.

— ✦ —

Earlier in the week — I mention this only because the thing deserves preservation in the record — Adi Singapore had posted a track in honour of Cinco de Mayo, the Mexican commemoration of the Battle of Puebla. Parama, who is to musical references what a champion bloodhound is to a fox's spoor, replied: Day of the Dead? And linked to the Grateful Dead's gig of May 5th, 1965. Sanjay then enquired, in the tone of a man with only the dimmest grasp of a foreign language: A brewer? Reader, I read this exchange three times before I could bring myself to type a response. It is the kind of clean, glittering joke one despairs of ever managing oneself. Sanjay, take a bow.

— ✦ —

Bhavani Shankar, an estimable cove of the old school, announced midweek that he was off to Liverpool to tick a particular item off his bucket list — the final bow of one P. Simon, formerly of Simon & Garfunkel, last chance, and so on. The rest of us pressed our faces to the railings. He returned with a dispatch from the front. Simon was a bit frail and his voice was shaky, but the spirit was undimmed. He, Bhavani, was not far from tearing up during "The Boxer," at which point any reasonable Englishman would have permitted himself a discreet honk into the handkerchief. So far so creditable. But here, dear reader, comes the moment I shall press to my heart for years to come. Bhavani reports that he was seething at some heathens behind me who ate potato chips noisily throughout Seven Psalms. I was almost expecting shouts of 'Freebird' from them. This, as one might say, set the hares running. Parama observed the cardinal rule: you play Freebird only when no one asks for it. Suman recalled that in mid-nineties Calcutta the cry had mutated and become Play November Rain! — a fate one would not wish upon one's chief enemy. The general thrust of the matter was that potato-chip-eating heathens at the back of a Paul Simon concert constitute a class of person not to be borne, and the world should know it.

— ✦ —

Parama, in his unofficial role as the chat's procurer of obscurities, also posted a video of a Tuareg band performing with admirable precision and absolutely no acknowledgement whatever from the audience, who looked to a man as though they had each privately suffered a small reversal of fortune just before the cameras began rolling. Suman, observing: Tough crowd! That band has clearly rehearsed the shit out of that track, but poker faces abound. Even Wasim Akram's lesser known Libyan cousin in the front row seems unimpressed. This, to my mind, is the finest sentence the chat has produced in months, possibly the year. Hari Menon, surfacing from Ireland, took up the theme: it reminded him of engineering school, same dudes looking like they'd smoked a few, same audience sex ratio too. The bassist and drummer, in Hari's elegant phrase, just took the word "pocket" away from everyone else. So one cannot say the music suffered. Only the spectators.

— ✦ —

Three notable losses came across the wire. Alex Ligertwood, vocalist on Santana's Zebop — gone at seventy-nine. Steve Cropper, the great Booker T sidekick, completed a final album before his passing, in what Adi described, with his characteristic precision, as one of them Last leaf type stories. And Nico Francis, drummer of a Pune-based outfit called Strangers — Hariharan's first live rock band, possibly anybody's — had also taken his exit. The chat marked all three the way the chat marks such things: a track, a tribute, a few honest words, and then on. It is the only seemly response one knows of.

— ✦ —

And then, late on Sunday, came a quieter post from Mapper. He had been sitting beside an ICU bed for the last 2 weeks tending to my father. A particular song, a particular version of a particular song, had been very comforting in these tough times. There is no embroidering this. The chat, which can banter and quip and trade puns about brewers with the best of them, also does this — receives such a message with the right kind of quiet, sends back the right kind of warmth, and lets the man have his evening. It is one of the things one likes about the place.

The Claude works in mysterious ways.

I. The Time-Out File

The lamplighters of the chat — those who keep watch through the small hours, the men whose names appear in the cable traffic at three in the morning when London is asleep and Singapore is just stirring — had been working an old file all week. It was called Take Five, and it had been a quiet matter for sixty-seven years until Sujit reopened it on the eve of Brubeck Day.

The official cover story, as everybody knew, was straightforward. Brubeck. Desmond. Morello on drums. New York, 1959. The 5/4 time signature was Desmond's, even if the credit on the label said only Brubeck. Case closed. Filed.

Except.

Sujit, who has the manner of an old hand who has seen one or two files reopened in his time, posted the lore. Brubeck's '58 tour of India. A late-night session in Bombay or possibly Calcutta — the records are unclear, the way records always are unclear when something interesting has happened. Joe Morello, the drummer, jamming with Leslie Godinho, a name that meant nothing to most of the chat and everything to those who knew. The 5/4 groove, perhaps, walking out of India in Morello's hands and surfacing a year later in a New York studio.

Ravi, who has a Joe in Berlin's distrust of any romantic legend, came in with the counter-brief. A scholarly piece on Subramania Pillai and the 5/4 rhythms of South Indian classical tradition that long pre-date Bombay jam sessions. The more plausible reason, he said, in the tone of a man who has read the file twice.

Sujit didn't yield. Louis Banks himself believes Godinho. There is an article. Possibly, he conceded — the operative word, the only honest word in any intelligence assessment — however.

The thing, you understand, will never be resolved. That is the nature of the thing. Two stories, both true in their way, neither one a lie, and somewhere between them the song.

— ✦ —

II. The Withers Dossier

Raj filed a separate report, equally well-sourced, on Bill Withers. The cover photograph on Just As I Am, 1971: Withers holding what appears to be his lunchbox. Because he was, at that moment, still working the assembly line at Weber Aircraft, fitting toilet seats into 747s. He was thirty-three. He had recorded an album. He was going to work in the morning.

It was Booker T. Jones who told him to start. The song "Grandma's Hands" is, in part, about that conversation. Booker T. produced the record. Played the keys.

Raj, with a courier's careful disclaimer: Now don't nobody be going I know I know I know I know at me, ok?

Suranjan, watching the back-channel: Well he did say Just As I Am.

It is the kind of small, perfect exchange that is the actual content of the chat — not the music, never quite the music, but the room in which the music is being passed around.

— ✦ —

III. The Guitarists' Dead-Letter Drop

Arjun took the late shift, as he often does when something has him by the throat. Six in the morning Singapore time, posting steadily into the silence: Allan Holdsworth at a smoking break in LA in 2009, lighting his first of two Wills Filter Navy Cuts, the photograph passed along like a piece of microfilm. (Holdsworth looks like an older and cooler version of Justin Trudeau, Robu observed, which is the kind of thing one notices at four in the morning and would never say in daylight.) Then Steve Howe. Then Steve Swallow with Wayne Krantz. Then a fretless guitarist whose name Arjun did not give and which one had to look up. Then free-form drums. Then — because every long night has its closing signal — Garcia. Eod Garcia dose, goodnight all ye 🌹.

A man who posts a rose at five in the morning is not asking for a reply. He is signing off.

— ✦ —

IV. The Dead and the Living

Alan Ligertwood is gone. Bharath flagged it — the Average White Band singer whose face, until DD's Live Aid telecast in 1985, was just a voice on the radio. He was a great vocalist. The chat marked the loss the way the chat marks all losses: a link, a note, a continuation.

Maynard Ferguson, on the other hand, would have been ninety-eight on the 4th. Sujit posted his cover of a standard, in the spirit of a man who keeps the calendar of trumpet players the way other men keep the calendar of saints.

And the great drummers were all over the file. Bill Kreutzmann of the Dead, whom Trey Anastasio described as the heartbeat — Mickey too. Together, they are one heart. Joe Morello with his disputed 5/4. Billy Cobham, whom Arjun called possessed of swang, which is its own word and needs no translation.

— ✦ —

V. Sujit's Question

At 1:30 in the morning, after the Brubeck files had been argued back and forth, Sujit posted a small image of writing on a page and asked simply: Any idea who wrote this?

Nobody answered before the week closed.

That, too, is the nature of the work. Some questions go into the file and stay there. The chat will come back to it, or it won't. Possibly, one of them will say, weeks from now. However.

The Claude works in mysterious ways.

Real Life Rock Top 10
In the manner of Greil Marcus

1. Bob Dylan, "Every Grain of Sand" / "All Along the Watchtower" — Asheville, NC, April 20

Robu and his friend Sundy, who introduced him to Dylan in a New England high school in the mid-1980s along with Adi Singapore and Arjun Mukerjee, finally got front-row seats forty years later. Robu's report from the floor: like holding infinity in the palm of your hand, even if only for a moment. "Every Grain of Sand," from Shot of Love (1981), is the hymn Dylan didn't admit was a hymn — written when he was forty, in the middle of what nobody yet realized was the long second act. The point of a front-row seat is that nothing mediates the song. The point of forty years is that everything does.

— ✦ —

2. Dave Mason, 1946–2026

Traffic's co-founder died on April 21. Three days later, Adi Singapore posted the story Mason told Guitar World in 2015. He and Hendrix were at a flat in London. John Wesley Harding had just come out. They put it on. Hendrix said: That's the coolest song! I'm gonna go and record it! Mason picked up the 12-string and went with him. The version of "All Along the Watchtower" Dylan now performs as if it were always Hendrix's song — because at some point it became Hendrix's song — has Dave Mason on it. He never got the credit. He gets it this week.

— ✦ —

3. Wayne Moss, 1937–2026

Nashville session guitarist. Hari Menon Ireland flagged the news; Parama responded immediately: Oh man, Wayne Moss's guitar on Just Like A Woman. The figure between the verses is as recognizable a lick as anything in American popular music — three seconds of guitar that you have heard, whether you knew it or not, every time that song has played anywhere for sixty years. That's a life.

— ✦ —

4. Michael Tilson Thomas, 1944–2026

Worked with Stravinsky, George Martin, and John McLaughlin — a triangulation that tells you everything. Bharath remembered first hearing him on Vision Is a Naked Sword (1971). Robu, who saw him conduct the SF Symphony many times: We have lost a titan.

— ✦ —

5. Charles Mingus, born April 22, 1922

Raj posts the cover: Mingus Mingus Mingus Mingus Mingus. Ravi: Mingus Ah Um. Raj: it's the man's birthday, not the album's. Ravi: Hence the Ah Um. Tufan, in tribute, composes a poem about a jazz cat who did not scat / Did not catch a rat. Sujit produces a first mono pressing tracked down in a Lucknow bylane. This is how a private music group observes a dead bassist's birthday: by arguing about which of his records is the right one.

— ✦ —

6. "Original Faubus Fables" (Candid, 1960)

Tufan posts the full lyric. Columbia refused the words in 1959 — too political, naming a sitting governor — so Mingus Ah Um got an instrumental. Mingus took it to Candid the next year and cut it the way he meant it: Dannie Richmond on call-and-response, the senators named like a roll of shame. Bilbo, Thomas, Faubus, Russell, Rockefeller, Byrd, Eisenhower. They brainwash and teach you hate. It still works because the names still rhyme with the present.

— ✦ —

7. Joni Mitchell with Charles Mingus, on his deathbed

Robu links the Mingus & Joni library piece, then a clip of Mingus himself, in a wheelchair, saying he wants to be buried by the Vedanta Society in India. He died in Cuernavaca; his ashes were scattered in the Ganges. American music keeps wandering off the map. It always did.

— ✦ —

8. Billy Cobham, Spectrum (Atlantic, 1973), "Taurian Matador," 1:40 mark

Anin tells the story. Tommy Bolin breaks his high E string mid-solo. Doesn't stop. Adjusts. The take stays on the record. Spectrum topped the Billboard jazz chart and cracked the 200. Today every recorded note has been edited, tuned, replaced, and replaced again before you hear it. That broken string is the difference.

— ✦ —

9. Saint Cecilia, patron saint of music

Saugata sets a quiz: what is common between Simon and Garfunkel and patron saints? Clue: took Gregory's place in popularity, but not vocally. Tufan, escalating: an imaginary album, Rhythm of the Patron Saints of Garfunkel and Simon. A song titled Tequilamockingbird Morning Has Broken. Then: Oh when the Patron Saints go marching in. Adrian eventually answers via DM: Cecilia. She bested Gregory. She lent her name to the song. The clue was hard, the answer is right, and it took until Saturday morning before Saugata told Tufan he could get off his knees.

— ✦ —

10. "Frankie and Johnny" — c. 1899 to present

Tufan, ear-wormed, builds a playlist of 250-plus versions: Dylan, Presley, Cash, Sam Cooke, on and on, all the way back to a 1928 proto-version he eventually links. The song is older than the Library of Congress's recordings of it. He was her man, and he done her wrong. The point isn't any one version. The point is that the song has been alive for 127 years and won't stop.

— ✦ —

Bonus: ARMS Concert, Royal Albert Hall, September 1983

Saugata revisits the Ronnie Lane MS benefit on grainy, snow-stormy VHS as he first saw it in Madras: Clapton, Winwood, Wyman, Watts, Beck, Page in his low-life phase, Ray Cooper, the lot. The back-lighting, the stage angles, the clothes — all reminiscent of The Last Waltz. Of course they are. The Last Waltz taught everybody what an ending is supposed to look like, and forty-two years later most of the people on that stage are still alive, and the people who watched them on Madras VHS are writing about it on WhatsApp from Singapore. That's also the secret history of rock and roll: the audience grew up, and kept listening.

Week 17 · 20–26 April 2026 · — after Greil Marcus, Real Life Rock Top 10

Week 16 13–19 April 2026
Tape Number One Is Gone. Everything Else Is Here.
Two voices this week
✦ Sanctorum
In the manner of Salman Rushdie
"In the beginning was the tape, and the tape was numbered, and the number was written in a ledger, and the ledger was kept by a woman for forty years without being asked, and when the woman died the ledger remained, and the tapes remained, and one Sunday night at midnight a man named Parama held up his grandfather's archive for a hundred and twenty-five people to see across the fiber-optic darkness..."

In the beginning was the tape, and the tape was numbered, and the number was written in a ledger, and the ledger was kept by a woman for forty years without being asked, and when the woman died the ledger remained, and the tapes remained, and one Sunday night at midnight a man named Parama held up his grandfather's archive for a hundred and twenty-five people to see across the fiber-optic darkness, and what they saw was not merely a cassette collection but a civilization — small, methodical, impeccably organized, two hundred magnetic reels each containing the ghost of a voice that had once filled a room in which a man shaved every morning to the sound of Carnatic music, because he understood, as certain men do, that music is not a luxury but a form of oxygen, and that to begin the day without it is to begin the day only partially alive.

The BASF tapes and the Philips tapes dated to the early seventies. The beginning. The low-noise Maxells marked the peak years — which anyone who has ever collected anything will recognize as the years when collecting becomes compulsion, when the shelves fill faster than the hours allow. And then the TDK workhorses, at the end, when the tapering began, because all things taper before they stop.

Tape number one was gone. Parama said so at 2:17 in the morning with the brevity of a person who has accepted a loss without entirely ceasing to grieve it. The group received this unanswerable question in the way it receives all unanswerable questions — with a moment of shared silence, and then more music, and then the conversation continuing, because the conversation must always continue, because the conversation is itself the thing.

— ✦ —

The same week, a man named Mike Westbrook died. Sujit documented the connections: Kathakali in 1978 at a festival in Italy, Westbrook hearing in the Kathakali dancer's movements the same impulse that had produced New Orleans, the Cortège of 1982, Gary Boyle from Patna, the London scene of the 1960s when certain cities became briefly permeable to every influence simultaneously. Parama said: amazing to read his connection between New Orleans jazz and Kathakali. Visualizing as I play. This is the correct response to the discovery that two things you love are secretly the same thing. The Indian Ocean shrinks to the width of a magnetic tape running from one spool to another.

— ✦ —

Now we must speak of Asha Bhosle, and of the song that was killed before it was born. Drumz brought the full history: Dum Maro Dum, 1970, a background song that was never supposed to exist, that became first a recording, then a controversy, then a ban, then twelve weeks at number one on Radio Ceylon, then a lakh people rising to their feet at concerts whenever the opening notes played, then Method Man and Busta Rhymes, then Kronos Quartet, then the iPhone 13 launch, then immortality. The government that bans a song hands it a gift it could not otherwise have received. The prohibition is the promotion. The silence it demands is the loudest advertisement available.

But before all of this, there was a woman who walked to a producer's house and stood there until he changed his mind. Usha Uthup says the vocals were hers first. Hariharan says he believes her, and will continue to believe her, because some stories are too consistent across too many decades on too many stages to be invention. Drumz offered the verdict: Asha is Dum Maro Dum. Usha is Ramba Ho. Asha Bhosle died this week knowing she had saved a song that would outlast her by centuries. There are worse things to know.

— ✦ —

In an auditorium in Madras — slow ceiling fans, the Ahuja PA system, a quiz that started two hours late — a microphone was switched on and emitted the characteristic feedback wail of the Ahuja at full power, and P2 stood up and said, in Tamil: Hendrix has come. Shall we go home? Adi Singapore added that he had been glad he stayed. As one generally is.

This same Hendrix had been playing, thirty years earlier, from a tape recorder in a dormitory room at IIT Madras, when Parama walked past the open door and said, simply: Hendrix. Manivannan, who owned the tape recorder, had heard in those two syllables a benediction — the recognition of a kindred soul, which is, in the vocabulary of this group, the highest available compliment.

— ✦ —
